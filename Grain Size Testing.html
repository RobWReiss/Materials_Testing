Two ways to measure grain size

Steel is made of tiny crystals called grains. Etching a polished sample with a weak acid shows the edges of the grains as dark lines. Grain size is given as a number, G. A bigger G means smaller grains. Both methods below give you G.

Why does grain size matter?

Strength and toughness
Finer grains make steel both stronger and tougher. This is why the tensile and Charpy results in the other modules depend on grain size.
How it is controlled
Grain size is set during steelmaking by the temperature of the steel while it is rolled and heat treated. Cooler finishing and added elements such as niobium keep the grains small.

1. Your specimen

In a real sample, the cut goes through grains at different heights, so some small shapes are only the tip of a bigger grain. A few small grains do not mean the steel is fine-grained. Judge by the typical grain, not the smallest.

2. Intercept method: count the crossings

  1. Count the grain boundaries that each orange line crosses. Each time a line passes from one grain into the next counts as one. Add up all three lines to get P.
  2. The three lines are each 622 µm long, so the total length is 3 × 622 = 1866 µm. (A µm is one thousandth of a millimetre. The scale bar shows 100 µm.)
  3. Average grain width: ℓ = 1866 ÷ P, in µm. Example: P = 30 gives 1866 ÷ 30 = 62.2 µm.
  4. Find the closest value of ℓ in the table below to read off G. In the example, 62.2 is closest to 56.3, so G = 5.

For the curious: G = −6.6457 × log₁₀(ℓ in mm) − 3.298.
